1 Introduction

Telephone-based customer service is one of the oldest and still most widely used channels of communication between companies and their clients. A call center is a centralized facility — physical or virtual — in which a group of operators (agents) handles large volumes of incoming and outgoing calls. Modern call centers rely on three pillars: an Interactive Voice Response (IVR) system that greets and routes callers automatically, queues that hold callers until an agent becomes available, and the agents themselves, who answer calls from their workstations using softphones or IP phones.

Traditionally, call center functionality was provided by expensive proprietary platforms (Cisco, Avaya, Genesys, …). Since the early 2000s, open-source telephony has changed this landscape. Asterisk, described by its authors as “the world's most popular open source communications project” [1], can be turned into a full call center platform: it natively supports IVR systems, call queues, agent logins, voicemail, call recording and integrations with external databases and applications through AGI, AMI and ARI [2].

The goal of this project is to design, configure and practically demonstrate a working call center based on Asterisk. The call center will feature a multi-level IVR menu driven by DTMF tones, one or more call queues with configurable distribution strategies, and several agents that can log in and out of queues dynamically. The whole solution is built exclusively with free, open-source software, which makes it reproducible at almost zero cost.

Throughout the documentation we explain every building block in detail (Asterisk's PJSIP endpoint configuration, the dialplan, queues.conf, the IVR structure and the AMI events used to supervise agent activity), describe the practical laboratory setup used for the demonstration, and evaluate the result from technical and practical points of view. Finally, we share our own opinion on the topic and present our own contribution to its elaboration.

Project scope in one sentence: build a small but complete call center — IVR, queues and agents — on a single Asterisk server, demonstrate it live with softphones and simulated calls, and document how every piece works.

2 State of the Art

2.1 From circuit-switched call centers to IP

The first call centers appeared in the 1970s and 1980s as rooms full of operators served by an Automatic Call Distributor (ACD) connected to a time-division multiplexing (TDM) private branch exchange (PBX). Calls were distributed by simple rules, and the integration with company databases — so-called Computer Telephony Integration (CTI) — was performed through proprietary links. These systems were effective, but expensive and rigid: capacity was fixed by hardware, the software was vendor-locked, and agents had to be physically present in the call center premises [3].

The convergence of voice and data networks in the early 2000s gave rise to the IP call center. Voice is transported as packets using VoIP protocols such as SIP [4], which removed the dependency on TDM hardware and allowed agents to work from anywhere with a broadband connection. The call center logic itself became software running on commodity servers — and this is precisely the niche into which open-source PBX projects emerged. Asterisk, first released by Mark Spencer in 2000, became the most widely deployed open-source communications platform and a common foundation for both PBXs and call centers [1].

2.2 The cloud and omnichannel era

Since the 2010s the industry has been shifting towards Contact Center as a Service (CCaaS): fully managed platforms such as Amazon Connect [5] or Genesys Cloud [6] are rented per agent per month instead of being owned. Alongside voice, modern systems unify email, web chat and messaging apps into a single agent desktop (omnichannel), and the COVID-19 pandemic greatly accelerated the adoption of fully remote agent teams.

The commercial licensing model, however, remains costly: large platforms charge tens of euros per agent every month, which for schools, small businesses, non-profit organizations and developing markets is often prohibitive. This economic gap is the main reason why open-source call center solutions remain relevant — they require only commodity hardware and offer essentially unlimited seats.

2.3 Open-source platforms

Several open-source projects address the call center domain. They can be grouped by the role they play in a VoIP infrastructure:

  • Asterisk — a full PBX and media server. Its app_queue module implements call queues with multiple distribution strategies, agents can log in and out dynamically, and IVR menus are built directly in the dialplan. Complete call center suites such as VICIdial [9] are built on top of it [2].
  • FreeSWITCH — a fork of Asterisk created in 2006 with a more modular architecture; it performs well under very high load, but is generally considered more complex to configure [7] [2].
  • Kamailio / OpenSIPS — SIP proxies (routers, not media servers) able to handle hundreds of thousands of simultaneous users. In large deployments they are often placed in front of Asterisk farms as a signaling tier, while Asterisk handles the media and the call center logic [8].
Platform Role Call center features Typical scale
Asterisk PBX / media server Native (queues, IVR, agents) SMEs to carriers
FreeSWITCH PBX / media server Native (modular) High-load platforms
Kamailio SIP proxy / router None (signaling only) Hundreds of thousands of users
Commercial (Genesys, Cisco) Turnkey suite Full omnichannel + AI Enterprise, pay per agent

2.4 Current trends

Two trends are currently reshaping the field. First, WebRTC turns the web browser into a full agent desktop: agents need no installed softphone, and as the course material states, “the website becomes the call center” [10] [11]. Gateways such as Asterisk can translate between WebRTC and SIP, which is exactly the technology behind modern browser-based agent consoles. Second, artificial intelligence (automatic speech recognition, real-time transcription, chatbots and sentiment-based routing) is moving from research into production — mostly in cloud platforms, but increasingly integrable with open-source stacks through external APIs.

Where our project fits: this project builds on Asterisk — the most established open-source platform — and implements on it the three pillars that every call center, cloud or on-premises, still relies on: an IVR, call queues and agents.

3 Technology & Configuration

3.1 Where the call center lives: Asterisk architecture

Asterisk consists of a core and a set of loadable modules (channel drivers, codecs and applications). The core reads the text configuration files from /etc/asterisk/, creates a channel for every call leg and executes the dialplan, the script that decides what happens to each call. Channels are finally joined through the bridging infrastructure [2]. For SIP endpoints we use the modern chan_pjsip driver; the older chan_sip is deprecated.

Four files are the heart of our lab: pjsip.conf (SIP accounts), extensions.conf (dialplan), queues.conf (call queues) and voicemail.conf. Changes are applied from the CLI with commands such as pjsip reload and queue reload all, and checked with pjsip show endpoints or queue show.

3.2 SIP endpoints: pjsip.conf

PJSIP is configured with small reusable objects: a transport (how SIP messages travel), an auth object (credentials), an AoR (Address of Record — where the endpoint can be reached), and an endpoint (the profile tying them together) [12]. Without an AoR, an endpoint cannot receive calls.

Listing 3.1 — Three endpoints in pjsip.conf (caller 1000, agents 2001 and 2002)

; /etc/asterisk/pjsip.conf (excerpt)
[transport-udp]
type=transport
protocol=udp
bind=0.0.0.0:5060

[auth-1000]
type=auth
auth_type=userpass
username=1000
password=Secret1000!

[aor-1000]
type=aor
max_contacts=1

[1000]
type=endpoint
transport=transport-udp
context=from-internal
disallow=all
allow=ulaw
auth=auth-1000
aors=aor-1000
callerid="Caller" <1000>

; --- Agent 2001 (agent 2002 is configured identically) ---
[auth-2001]
type=auth
auth_type=userpass
username=2001
password=Secret2001!

[aor-2001]
type=aor
max_contacts=1

[2001]
type=endpoint
transport=transport-udp
context=agent-control
disallow=all
allow=ulaw
auth=auth-2001
aors=aor-2001

Each softphone registers with its username and password (SIP Digest authentication, see §3.8). The context parameter decides which part of the dialplan handles calls coming from that endpoint: callers land in from-internal, agents in agent-control, where the login/logout codes live.

3.3 The dialplan: contexts, extensions and priorities

The dialplan is a small procedural language. It is divided into contexts; inside each context, an exten line matches a dialled number and runs a sequence of priorities (applications). The n priority means “next”. Patterns start with an underscore and use X (0–9), Z (1–9), N (2–9) and . (any length), as shown in the course slides [2]. Built-in variables such as ${EXTEN} and ${CALLERID(num)} make the dialplan dynamic.

Listing 3.2 — extensions.conf: basic routing

[from-internal]
; test extension: play a greeting
exten => 600,1,NoOp(Test call from ${CALLERID(num)})
 same => n,Playback(hello-world)
 same => n,Hangup()

; dial any 2xxx extension directly
exten => _2XXX,1,NoOp(Internal call to ${EXTEN})
 same => n,Dial(PJSIP/${EXTEN},20)
 same => n,Hangup()

; everything else goes to the IVR (most specific pattern wins)
exten => _.,1,Goto(ivr-main,s,1)

3.4 Designing the IVR

An IVR greets the caller and offers a menu of DTMF options. We use Answer() to establish the call, Progress() to signal early media (the “183 Session Progress” mechanism studied in lecture 4 [18]), and Background(), which plays a recording while still listening for DTMF digits. WaitExten(5) waits five seconds for a digit; t catches a timeout and i an invalid entry.

Our prompt (cc-welcome) is our own recording: “Welcome to the VoIP call center. Press 1 for Sales, 2 for Technical Support, or 0 for the operator.” Instead of hard-coding a destination per option, the menu stores the chosen queue in a channel variable (SELECTED_QUEUE) and jumps to a single queueing context.

Listing 3.3 — IVR menu in extensions.conf

[ivr-main]
exten => s,1,Answer()
 same => n,Wait(1)
 same => n,Progress()
 same => n,Set(SELECTED_QUEUE=sales)     ; default destination
 same => n,Background(cc-welcome)
 same => n,WaitExten(5)

exten => 1,1,Set(SELECTED_QUEUE=sales)
 same => n,Goto(call-queue,s,1)

exten => 2,1,Set(SELECTED_QUEUE=support)
 same => n,Goto(call-queue,s,1)

exten => 0,1,Goto(call-queue,s,1)        ; operator -> default queue
exten => t,1,Goto(call-queue,s,1)        ; no digit pressed
exten => i,1,Playback(invalid)
 same => n,Goto(s,1)

3.5 Queues and agents: queues.conf

The app_queue module is Asterisk's ACD. A queue holds callers in a waiting list, plays music on hold and periodic position announcements, and delivers each call to a free member using a strategy [13]. The most common strategies are:

  • ringall — ring every available member at once (first to answer wins);
  • leastrecent — ring the member least recently called;
  • fewestcalls — ring the member with the fewest answered calls;
  • rrmemory — round-robin, remembering where the last call was dispatched;
  • random — choose a member at random.

timeout and retry control how long a member is rung before the agent is skipped; maxlen limits the queue size; announce-frequency and periodic-announce produce the “your call is important to us” messages. Members can be declared statically in queues.conf (our demo) or added dynamically with AddQueueMember().

Listing 3.4 — Two queues in queues.conf

; /etc/asterisk/queues.conf (excerpt)
[general]
persistentmembers=yes

[sales]
strategy=ringall
timeout=15
retry=5
maxlen=10
joinannounce=cc-you-are-next
announce-frequency=30
periodic-announce-frequency=60
periodic-announce=cc-please-hold
musicclass=default
member => PJSIP/2001,0,Agent-2001
member => PJSIP/2002,0,Agent-2002

[support]
strategy=leastrecent
timeout=20
retry=5
maxlen=10
musicclass=default
member => PJSIP/2002,0,Agent-2002

For a more realistic setup, agents can log in and out themselves: dialling *88 adds their own PJSIP channel to the queue and *89 removes it (${CHANNEL(peername)} gives us the current endpoint name). These two codes live in the agent-control context assigned to agents in Listing 3.1. In that case the static member => lines must be removed to avoid duplicates.

Listing 3.5 — Dynamic agent login/logout

[agent-control]
exten => *88,1,Set(MEMBER=PJSIP/${CHANNEL(peername)})
 same => n,AddQueueMember(sales,${MEMBER})
 same => n,Playback(agent-loginok)
 same => n,Hangup()

exten => *89,1,RemoveQueueMember(sales,PJSIP/${CHANNEL(peername)})
 same => n,Playback(agent-loggedoff)
 same => n,Hangup()

3.6 Queueing the caller

The Queue() application puts the caller into the queue chosen by the IVR. Its last argument is a timeout (120 seconds here); when the timeout expires, dialplan execution continues with the next priority, where we play an apology and offer voicemail [14]. While waiting, the caller hears music on hold and the periodic announcements configured in Listing 3.4.

Listing 3.6 — The queueing context

[call-queue]
exten => s,1,Progress()
 same => n,Queue(${SELECTED_QUEUE},,,,120)
 same => n,Playback(cc-no-agent-available)
 same => n,VoiceMail(1000@voip)
 same => n,Hangup()

The voicemail.conf mailbox is declared as 1000 => 1212, Call Center, cc@example.org; the same credential is used by the endpoint that owns the mailbox [2].

3.7 Monitoring: CDR and AMI

Asterisk writes a Call Detail Record (CDR) for every call: source, destination, context, start/answer/end times, duration, billsec and disposition (ANSWERED, NO ANSWER, BUSY, FAILED). By default the records are CSV files in /var/log/asterisk/cdr-csv/ [15]. They are the basis for the statistics in section 4.

For live supervision, the Asterisk Manager Interface (AMI) exposes commands and events over a TCP socket using simple Key: Value pairs [16]. Events of type Agent (for example AgentCalled, AgentConnect, AgentComplete) and queue events (QueueMemberAdded, QueueMemberRemoved) let an external program build a wallboard showing agent states in real time. A minimal session looks like this:

Listing 3.7 — AMI session (login + queue status)

Action: Login
Username: admin
Secret: MySecret

Action: QueueStatus
Queue: sales

Complementary interfaces are AGI, which invokes an external script (in any language) from the dialplan to consult a CRM/database, and ARI, a REST API for building completely custom communication applications [2].

3.8 Security considerations

Our extensions authenticate with SIP Digest (a challenge/response mechanism that hashes the password, with 401 Unauthorized or 407 Proxy Authentication Required responses). For production use, lecture 13 [17] recommends adding a TLS transport (protocol=tls, with a certificate signed by a trusted CA) so signaling is encrypted hop by hop, plus SRTP for the media stream. IP-based ACLs in pjsip.conf can additionally restrict which addresses are allowed to register.

3.9 The complete call flow

Putting all the pieces together, one call through our call center follows these steps:

  1. The caller's Linphone registers as extension 1000 and dials the call center number.
  2. PJSIP creates a channel and the from-internal dialplan sends the call to ivr-main.
  3. Asterisk answers, plays the welcome prompt and waits for a DTMF digit.
  4. The chosen option sets SELECTED_QUEUE (sales or support) and jumps to call-queue.
  5. Queue() places the caller on hold, plays announcements and rings free agents with the configured strategy.
  6. An agent answers on extension 2001/2002; Asterisk bridges both channels and AMI reports an AgentConnect event.
  7. Either party hangs up; the bridge is destroyed and a CDR with duration and disposition is written.
In short: PJSIP handles who can call, the dialplan handles where the call goes, the IVR handles what the caller wants, queues.conf handles who answers, and CDR/AMI handle what happened. Section 4 demonstrates the whole chain live.

4 Practical Demonstration

4.1 Laboratory topology

The lab runs on a single physical computer with a dual-boot Windows/Linux installation. Windows stays as the working environment and hosts VirtualBox; inside it, an Ubuntu Desktop 24.04 LTS virtual machine runs Asterisk. The virtual network adapter is configured in bridged mode, so the VM and the Windows host share the same LAN segment and can reach each other directly — no NAT traversal is needed. Linphone runs on Windows as the caller (extension 1000) and as the two agents (2001 and 2002); additional callers can be registered from mobile phones on the same Wi-Fi network.

Figure 4.1 — Topology of the laboratory setup (example addresses)

   Windows host (192.168.1.20)                Ubuntu VM - bridged (192.168.1.50)
   ┌────────────────────────────┐             ┌────────────────────────────────┐
   │ Linphone 1000  (caller)    │  UDP 5060   │ Asterisk 20 (PJSIP)            │
   │ Linphone 2001  (agent)     │ ──────────► │  · pjsip.conf                  │
   │ Linphone 2002  (agent)     │  RTP        │  · extensions.conf + IVR       │
   │ Wireshark (capture)        │ ◄────────── │  · queues.conf (sales/support) │
   │ Web browser (this website) │  20000+     │  · CDR + AMI                   │
   └────────────────────────────┘             └────────────────────────────────┘

The addresses above are examples: the real address of the VM is discovered with ip addr show (look for eth0/enp0s3) and must be written into Linphone's domain field and into the SIPp command later.

4.2 Building the server

  1. Install VirtualBox on Windows and create a VM with 2 CPUs, 4 GB RAM, a 25 GB virtual disk and an Ubuntu Desktop 24.04 LTS image.
  2. In the VM settings set Network → Adapter 1 → Attached to: Bridged Adapter. This is the single most important setting for SIP to work.
  3. Install Ubuntu and, in the VM, run: 
    sudo apt update
sudo apt install asterisk asterisk-sounds-en espeak-ng sox sip-tester tshark
    The package sip-tester provides SIPp (lecture 6 [19]) and tshark is the command-line Wireshark.
  4. Take a VirtualBox snapshot here (“clean install”) — it lets us return to a working state if a later configuration breaks the server.
  5. Check the installation: 
    sudo systemctl status asterisk
sudo asterisk -rvvv     # Asterisk CLI: 'core show version', 'pjsip show endpoints'
exit

4.3 Configuration files and voice prompts

The definitive configuration files used in this lab are shipped with this website in the website/lab/ folder: pjsip.conf, extensions.conf, queues.conf, voicemail.conf and a short lab guide. They are copies of Listings 3.1–3.6 with the example IP addresses adapted. Copy them into the VM and reload: 

sudo cp ~/lab/pjsip.conf ~/lab/extensions.conf ~/lab/queues.conf \
        ~/lab/voicemail.conf /etc/asterisk/
sudo chown asterisk:asterisk /etc/asterisk/{pjsip,extensions,queues,voicemail}.conf
sudo asterisk -rx "core reload"
sudo asterisk -rx "pjsip reload"
sudo asterisk -rx "queue reload all"

Instead of using copyright-licensed music or generic system recordings, our IVR prompts are generated with the open-source speech synthesizer eSpeak-ng and resampled to the 8 kHz mono format Asterisk expects, using sox: 

espeak-ng -w /tmp/welcome.wav -s 135 \
  "Welcome to the VoIP call center. Press 1 for Sales, 2 for Technical Support, or 0 for the operator."
sox /tmp/welcome.wav -r 8000 -c 1 -b 16 /tmp/cc-welcome.wav
sudo cp /tmp/cc-welcome.wav /var/lib/asterisk/sounds/en/
sudo chown asterisk:asterisk /var/lib/asterisk/sounds/en/cc-welcome.wav

The same procedure produces cc-you-are-next.wav, cc-please-hold.wav and cc-no-agent-available.wav. Other prompts (hello-world, invalid, agent-loginok) come from the asterisk-sounds-en package. After copying the prompts and the configs, take a second snapshot (“configured call center”).

4.4 Configuring Linphone

Open Linphone on Windows and add a SIP account for each participant:

  • Username: 1000 (caller), 2001 and 2002 (agents);
  • Password: the one from pjsip.conf (e.g. Secret1000!);
  • Domain: the VM's IP address (e.g. 192.168.1.50);
  • Transport: UDP, port 5060; codec: PCMU/G.711u (ulaw), matching allow=ulaw in the endpoint configuration.

In the Asterisk CLI, pjsip show contacts must list all three endpoints with a contact URI and status Avail. That confirms that registration and authentication are working before we place any call.

4.6 Traffic capture with Wireshark / tcpdump

To prove that the signaling and the media really traverse our server, we capture the traffic on the VM during a call. Because the adapter is bridged, Wireshark on the Windows host can see the same packets; we also capture on the server itself with tshark: 

sudo tshark -i eth0 -w /tmp/callcenter.pcap \
  -f "port 5060 or (udp portrange 20000-30000)"

In Wireshark, the display filter sip || rtp shows exactly the protocol sequence studied in class: REGISTER → 200 OK, INVITE → 100 Trying → 180 Ringing → 200 OK → ACK, the DTMF digits sent as RFC 2833 telephone-event packets [20], the RTP audio stream, and finally BYE → 200 OK. The Wireshark Telephony → RTP Streams view can even play back the captured conversation — a very strong piece of evidence for the demonstration.

4.7 Generating load with SIPp

To show that the queue behaves correctly under more than one call, we use SIPp [19] to generate several simultaneous callers. The default uac scenario is enough; digest credentials are passed with -au/-ap and the destination number 500 reaches our IVR: 

sipp -sn uac 192.168.1.50 -s 500 -au 1000 -ap 'Secret1000!' \
     -r 2 -rp 3000 -l 5 -m 10 -d 20000 -aa

This starts two calls per second, with at most five parallel calls, for ten calls total. While it runs, queue show sales shows callers waiting and agents busy; afterwards tail /var/log/asterisk/cdr-csv/Master.csv shows one CDR per generated call. This is the bridge between our lab and the monitoring concepts of lecture 11 [15].

4.9 Troubleshooting checklist

  • Endpoint does not register → check username/password in auth, the aor association and UDP 5060 reachability; use pjsip show contacts.
  • Call connects but no audio → check the firewall and the RTP port range in rtp.conf (default UDP 20000–30000), and that the VM uses bridged networking.
  • DTMF not recognized by the IVR → set dtmfmode=rfc2833 in the endpoint and verify the telephone-event packets in Wireshark [20].
  • A prompt is missing → reinstall asterisk-sounds-en or generate that prompt with eSpeak-ng, as in §4.3.
  • Agents do not ring → run queue show and check member states; a statically configured member and a dynamic AddQueueMember of the same extension must not coexist.

5 Opinion & Contribution

5.1 Our opinion on the topic

Working on this topic changed our view of call centers. Before the project we saw them as old-fashioned rooms full of headsets; after configuring one ourselves we realized that the logic is what matters, not the hardware. Even in 2026, when chat bots and messaging apps are everywhere, a voice call is still the fastest way to resolve an urgent or complex problem, and the same three mechanisms — menu, queue, agent — are present in cloud platforms costing hundreds of euros per agent per month.

Our opinion on Asterisk is two-sided. On the positive side, it is extremely complete: PJSIP, the dialplan, app_queue, voicemail, CDR and AMI all worked together in a single application, without buying any licence. On the negative side, the learning curve is steep: the same name can be reused for different PJSIP object types, a single typo in the dialplan silently changes the call flow, and Asterisk has no graphical interface by default (FreePBX exists, but we intentionally configured everything by hand to understand what happens underneath). Debugging with asterisk -rvvv and Wireshark was essential — and educational.

We are also aware of the limits of our implementation. One Asterisk instance is a single point of failure; the built-in queues do not provide omnichannel routing, AI-based prioritization or detailed analytics; and CDR files are only raw data, not reports. For a production contact center, a redundant cluster, a database-backed agent directory and a monitoring stack such as HOMER [11] would be needed. Nevertheless, for learning and for a small organization, we consider open-source Asterisk an excellent choice.

5.2 Our contribution to the elaboration of the project

The assignment explicitly asks for our own contribution to the elaboration of the project. Our work goes beyond following a configuration tutorial in the following points:

  1. A unified IVR-to-queue design. Instead of creating a separate dialplan branch for every menu option, the IVR stores the destination in the channel variable SELECTED_QUEUE and hands all calls to one call-queue context. Adding a fourth department later requires changing only the variable value in one line.
  2. Agent self-service login/logout. The *88/*89 codes derive the member interface from ${CHANNEL(peername)}, so any agent extension can join or leave the queue without editing queues.conf — a small but practical, reproduction-friendly detail.
  3. Fully original voice prompts. The IVR recordings are generated with eSpeak-ng and resampled with sox (§4.3). No copyrighted audio library is used, and the whole prompt set can be regenerated from a single command.
  4. A reproducible validation method. We did not limit ourselves to a manual call: SIPp generates controlled load against the queue, while CDR, queue show and a Wireshark RTP capture provide three independent views of the same calls. Combining these tools comes directly from the course material [15] [19] and, as far as we know, is not presented as a single workflow in any of the lectures.
  5. A comparison of queue strategies in practice. Two queues use different strategies (ringall for Sales, leastrecent for Support) so their behaviour can be compared during the same demonstration, instead of merely listing the strategies in theory.
  6. Documentation as a deliverable. The configuration files are shipped together with this website, and every step of the lab is documented with expected output and a troubleshooting checklist, so the project can be reproduced by another student without assistance.

5.3 What we learned

Technically, we learned how SIP registration and authentication work in practice, how to build a dialplan and an IVR, how call queues distribute calls and how CDR and AMI expose what happened inside Asterisk. We also practised network analysis with Wireshark and traffic generation with SIPp. Academically, the most valuable lesson was understanding why the protocols studied in the first half of the course — SDP offer/answer, early media, DTMF transport, RTP — are not abstract theory but the mechanisms that make a queue announcement or a menu press work.

6 Conclusion

This project has shown that a functional call center — with an IVR menu, two call queues and dynamically manageable agents — can be built entirely with open-source software on a single Ubuntu virtual machine. The demonstration confirmed that the whole chain works: a caller reaches the menu, DTMF input selects a department, the queue rings free agents, the conversation is bridged, and every call leaves a CDR record that can be used for statistics. Traffic captures verified the SIP and RTP behaviour predicted by the theory.

The solution is not intended to replace a carrier-grade contact center: it lacks redundancy, omnichannel support and advanced analytics. Its value lies elsewhere — it is free, transparent, reproducible and small enough to be fully understood by the people who operate it. For a semestral project it offered the right balance between theory and practice, and it connected most of the topics covered during the semester: SIP, RTP, codecs, early media, Asterisk configuration, quality of service and monitoring.

As future work we would consider connecting Linphone directly from the browser through WebRTC [7], storing agents and queue rules in a database, adding TLS/SRTP encryption [17], and building a small web dashboard fed by AMI events [16] to display agent states in real time. These extensions would turn the laboratory setup into a system closer to what commercial environments use today, while keeping the open-source foundation intact.

Final statement: the objectives of the assignment have been met — a selected technology was described in detail, a working practical demonstration was carried out, and the resulting documentation is published as this website.
